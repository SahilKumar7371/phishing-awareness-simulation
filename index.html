Notice anything odd already?

  • Urgency — a tight deadline pressures quick action
  • Fear — threat of suspension or data loss
  • Authority — impersonates "IT Support"
  • Mismatched sender domain
  • A call-to-action button instead of a real link you can inspect

⚠️ What actually happens if this were real

None of this happened here — but on a genuine phishing attempt, here's what clicking through and entering your information can lead to:

  • Account takeover — the attacker signs in as you and locks you out by changing the password/recovery details
  • Credential stuffing on other sites — if you reuse passwords, attackers try the same login on your email, banking, and social accounts
  • Financial loss — unauthorized purchases, wire transfers, or drained accounts if banking/payment info is involved
  • Identity theft — enough personal data can be used to open accounts or loans in your name
  • Malware or ransomware installation — some fake pages silently install malicious software just by loading, or via a fake attachment/app prompt
  • Your contacts get targeted next — a compromised account is often used to send phishing messages to everyone you know
  • Organization-wide breach — one compromised work account can give attackers a foothold into an entire company network

Extra risk specific to this technique

    This is exactly why the safest reaction to a suspicious message is to not click, not enter anything, and report it — not to "test" whether it's real.

    Awareness Training

    Phishing Awareness Training

    What is phishing?

    Phishing is a social-engineering attack where someone impersonates a trusted source — a company, coworker, or system — to trick you into handing over information or taking an action you otherwise wouldn't, like entering a password on a fake site.

    Phishing techniques — the full picture

    "Phishing" covers a whole family of attacks. They all lean on the same trick — impersonating someone or something you trust — but they show up in very different places.

    • Email phishing — generic fraudulent emails blasted to many people at once, like the one you just saw
    • Spear phishing — a targeted email personalized with your name, role, or company details to feel more convincing
    • Whaling — spear phishing aimed at executives or high-value targets, often impersonating a CEO or board member
    • Clone phishing — a copy of a real email you've received before, with the link or attachment swapped for a malicious one
    • Business Email Compromise (BEC) — attacker impersonates a colleague or vendor to request a wire transfer or gift cards
    • Smishing — phishing via SMS/text, e.g. a fake "delivery failed" or "bank alert" text with a link
    • Vishing — phishing over a phone call, often impersonating tech support, a bank, or the police
    • Pharming — redirects you to a fake site even when you type the correct address, via DNS or malware
    • Quishing — phishing via a malicious QR code that leads to a fake login page
    • Angler phishing — a fake "customer support" account on social media that DMs you after you complain about a brand

    Warning signs

    • Urgent or threatening messages
    • Suspicious or shortened links
    • Unknown or mismatched senders
    • Unexpected attachments
    • Requests for passwords or codes
    • Fake or look-alike login pages
    • Unexpected QR codes on posters, emails, or parking meters
    • Unsolicited calls asking you to "verify" account details

    ⚠️ The real risk if you click and enter credentials

    • Account takeover — attacker locks you out by resetting your password/recovery info
    • Credential stuffing — reused passwords let them into your email, banking, and social accounts too
    • Financial loss — unauthorized charges, transfers, or drained accounts
    • Identity theft — enough data to open accounts or loans in your name
    • Malware/ransomware — some fake pages or attachments install malicious software on your device
    • Your contacts get targeted next — a hijacked account is used to phish everyone in your address book
    • Organizational breach — one clicked work-account link can expose an entire company network

    How to stay safe

    • Check the sender's actual email address, not just the display name
    • Hover or check the real destination URL before clicking
    • Never share passwords or one-time codes over email, text, or phone
    • Avoid clicking links in unexpected "urgent" messages, and avoid scanning unexpected QR codes
    • Verify unexpected requests through a separate, trusted channel (call the company back on a known number)
    • Use unique passwords per site and a password manager so one leak can't cascade everywhere
    • Turn on multi-factor authentication so a stolen password alone isn't enough
    • Report suspicious messages to IT/security instead of ignoring them
